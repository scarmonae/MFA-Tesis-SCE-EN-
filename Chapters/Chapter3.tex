% \noindent En el marco del Proyecto C3, una colaboración interdisciplinaria entre la Universidad EAFIT (Colombia) y la Real Universidad de Tecnología de Estocolmo (KTH, Suecia), se planteó la necesidad de desarrollar un sistema de carga inteligente para un vehículo aéreo no tripulado (UAV) orientado al monitoreo ambiental de la biodiversidad y la salud de los ecosistemas en Colombia. Mientras KTH lideró el diseño estructural y aerodinámico de la plataforma UAV, el equipo de EAFIT asumió la responsabilidad de la procura y construcción de la carga, posteriormente denominada HuMath\textregistered QBee.\\

% \noindent El proceso de procura comenzó con la identificación de proveedores de sensores ópticos y electrónica de adquisición de datos con características adecuadas para capturar imágenes multiespectrales en rangos visible y cercano al infrarrojo. El financiamiento para la adquisición de estos componentes fue gestionado a través del proyecto 4DAir, una iniciativa de Minciencias dirigida por la Profesora Elena Montilla Rosero, que permitió asegurar recursos para la compra de sensores, objetivos, filtros y hardware de control. Asimismo, Saab AB, proporcionó fondos para la fabricación de la estructura de la aeronave y supervisó la gestión administrativa y logística del proyecto.\\

% \noindent Inicialmente, se había previsto subcontratar la manufactura de la plataforma a una empresa colombiana especializada; sin embargo, limitaciones tecnológicas y de capacidad obligaron a que el equipo de ingeniería de EAFIT, junto con voluntarios de los programas de Ingeniería Aeronáutica de la Universidad Pontificia Bolivariana y de Ingeniería de la Universidad EAFIT, asumiera la fabricación de la estructura del UAV. Este esfuerzo colaborativo garantizó la integración eficiente entre la plataforma aérea y el sistema de carga HuMath\textregistered QBee.\\

% % \noindent La construcción del HuMath\textregistered QBee involucró etapas iterativas de ensamblaje mecánico, integración de sensores y desarrollo de software de control. Primero se diseñaron y fabricaron alojamientos impresos en resina para los sistemas ópticos NIR y VIS, asegurando alineación y modularidad. Posteriormente, se integraron los sensores Alvium 1800 U-501m y U-500c de Allied Vision, junto con sus objetivos y filtros, sobre un chasis impreso en PET-G. Paralelamente, se implementó un script en Python para la adquisición sincronizada de datos multiespectrales y georreferenciación en tiempo real.\\

\chapter{Procurement and construction of the HuMath\textregistered\ QBee Device}
\label{Chapter3} 

\noindent Within the framework of the C3 Project, an interdisciplinary collaboration between Universidad EAFIT (Colombia) and the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm (KTH, Sweden), the need arose to develop an intelligent payload system for an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) aimed at environmental monitoring of biodiversity and ecosystem health in Colombia. While KTH led the structural and aerodynamic design of the UAV platform, the EAFIT team took responsibility for the procurement and construction of the payload, later named HuMath\textregistered\ QBee.\\

\noindent The procurement process began with the identification of suppliers of optical sensors and data acquisition electronics with characteristics suitable for capturing multispectral images in the visible and near-infrared ranges. Funding for the acquisition of these components was managed through the 4DAir project, a Minciencias initiative led by Professor Elena Montilla Rosero, which made it possible to secure resources for the purchase of sensors, lenses, filters, and control hardware. Likewise, Saab AB provided funds for the manufacture of the aircraft structure and oversaw the project’s administrative and logistical management.\\

\noindent Initially, subcontracting the manufacture of the platform to a specialized Colombian company had been planned; however, technological and capacity limitations meant that EAFIT’s engineering team, together with volunteers from the Aeronautical Engineering program at the Pontifical Bolivarian University and from the Engineering program at Universidad EAFIT, undertook fabrication of the UAV structure. This collaborative effort ensured efficient integration between the aerial platform and the HuMath\textregistered\ QBee payload system.\\

% \noindent The construction of the HuMath\textregistered\ QBee involved iterative stages of mechanical assembly, sensor integration, and control software development. First, resin-printed housings were designed and manufactured for the NIR and VIS optical systems, ensuring alignment and modularity. Subsequently, Allied Vision’s Alvium 1800 U-501m and U-500c sensors, along with their lenses and filters, were integrated on a PET-G printed chassis. In parallel, a Python script was implemented for synchronized acquisition of multispectral data and real-time georeferencing.\\

\noindent This chapter describes in detail each of the phases of procurement and construction of the HuMath\textregistered\ QBee, highlighting the technical decisions, component selection criteria, and collaboration strategies that enabled the materialization of this innovative environmental monitoring platform.\\

The QBee case\footnote{A substantial portion of the CAD design was carried out by the C3 project manufacturing team, led by Valeria Solorzano Araque and Lisseth Milena Cruz Ruiz. I gratefully acknowledge their work and dedication.} is printed in PET-G (polyethylene terephthalate modified with glycol), a polymer with a typical density around 1,317 g/cm³ that combines good impact resistance (Charpy $\approx$ 4,03 kJ/m²) and elongation at break up to 15\% in standard formulations. Its tensile modulus (~4015 MPa) and flexural modulus (~2987 MPa) ensure stiffness, while its glass transition temperature ($\approx$ 76 °C) and melting range (220–260 °C) make it stable under the heat generated by nearby electronics. In addition, it exhibits excellent chemical durability and minimal tendency to \emph{warping}, making it ideal for parts of complex geometry. The total weight of the device is 1,810 grams, thus meeting the imposed weight requirement.\\

\noindent The external dimensions of the case were designed to fit into the internal bay of the C3 UAV (see Figure~\ref{fig:qbee_dims} for the detailed envelope and mounting pattern), without requiring hermetic sealing, since it would be protected from external conditions. This system is coupled to a GPS HAT, which supplies the system with metadata such as date, UTC time, latitude, longitude, altitude, and elevation angle, essential for georeferencing and orthorectification of images. Finally, the in-situ pollutant sensing module shares a 5 V supply from the same battery and occupies the rear compartment of the housing, completing the QBee’s functionality.\\

\section{Data Acquisition and Control System}
\label{sec:script_control_adquisicion}

\noindent With the aim of automating the process of capturing, processing, and storing multispectral images during flights or bench tests, a script was developed in Python that enables simultaneous acquisition of images in the visible (VIS) and near-infrared (NIR) spectra, also integrating real-time global positioning (GPS) data.\\

\noindent This script is an adaptation of the example code provided by the \texttt{vmbpy} library, corresponding to the \textit{Allied Vision} SDK, and was substantially modified to include multithreading, conditional video writing, telemetry overlay, and keyboard control during execution. In addition, two system services were incorporated to (i) initialize and harden the GNSS/GPS configuration at boot, and (ii) synchronize the acquired videos to cloud storage automatically and \emph{post hoc}.\\

\subsection{General structure of the Script}

\noindent The logical flow of the code is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:diagrama_script}, highlighting three fundamental stages: (i) environment initialization and camera detection, (ii) concurrent frame acquisition via producer threads, and (iii) image processing and visualization via a consumer thread.\\

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[trim = 0 0 0 1cm, clip, width=1\textwidth]{Figures/C4/OSU_main.pdf}
    \caption{Logical flow of the QBee acquisition and visualization script. Multiple cameras, parallel acquisition, and real-time processing are integrated.}
    \label{fig:diagrama_script}
\end{figure}


\noindent During the initialization phase, a connection is established with the local GPS server (\texttt{gpsd}) using the library of the same name. In parallel, the \texttt{MainThread} class is instantiated, responsible for automatically detecting all cameras connected through the \texttt{VmbSystem}. Each detected camera is assigned to an independent thread of the \texttt{FrameProducer} class, which configures critical parameters such as acquisition resolution, pixel format (\texttt{Mono8} or \texttt{Bgr8}), and automatic exposure mode. These threads are responsible for capturing frames in real time, estimating frames per second (FPS), and enqueuing images into a FIFO structure (\texttt{queue.Queue}) for subsequent processing.\\


\noindent The core stage of the system is contained in the \texttt{FrameConsumer} class, which operates as the consumer thread. This class performs multiple tasks in parallel: (i) extracts frames from the shared queue, (ii) resizes images if the acquisition resolution does not match the expected \SI[parse-numbers = false]{2592 \times 1944}{px}, (iii) converts image formats to RGB if necessary, and (iv) concatenates images captured by multiple cameras into a single horizontal mosaic. On this composite image, an information bar (\textit{overlay}) is generated that includes real-time georeferenced data (latitude, longitude, altitude, speed, heading angle, and timestamp. See Figure \ref{fig:gps_overlay_visual}), in addition to the estimated frames per second for each camera. This overlay is constructed as an additional matrix that is stacked vertically onto the original image.\\

\noindent The system also allows direct interaction with the operator via specific keys to execution of the following commands:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{s}}: captures the current image and saves it in \texttt{JPEG} format.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{v}}: starts or stops video recording in \texttt{AVI} format, with \texttt{XVID} compression, at a frequency of 24 FPS.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{Enter}}: ends script execution and closes all windows.
\end{itemize}

\noindent To ensure autonomous operation and minimize user intervention, a Linux system service (\texttt{systemd}) executes the acquisition script automatically at boot on a Raspberry~Pi~4~Model~B (8\,GB RAM). Thanks to this configuration, the \texttt{QBee} system is activated as soon as the external battery is connected, without requiring a desktop session. Immediately after boot, the script starts image capture and begins storing images and videos in local storage.\\

\subsection{Deterministic GNSS configuration}
\label{subsec:gps_boot_service}

\noindent Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) is the umbrella term for satellite
navigation constellations—such as GPS (USA), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (EU), and
BeiDou (China)—that broadcast signals enabling precise Positioning, Navigation
and Timing (PNT) on or near Earth.\\

\noindent Every time the GPS loses power completely, its configuration is reset. To address this, a Linux service was developed to configure the GPS each time the system starts. This service ensures a deterministic and interference-resilient GNSS configuration at each boot, initializing the receiver and launching \texttt{gpsd} with the required serial and timing parameters. The service performs the following high-level sequence (see Figure~\ref{fig:gps_service_flow}). The service performs the following high-level sequence (see Figure~\ref{fig:gps_service_flow}):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Liberate the serial port:} stop any existing \texttt{gpsd} socket/service and kill stray processes; ensure \texttt{/dev/serial0} is not busy.
  \item \textbf{Factory-rate handshake:} set \texttt{/dev/serial0} to \SI{9600}{bps} (raw, 8N1) and open the port to speak to the GNSS at its factory baud.
  \item \textbf{Re-rate and configure:} switch the receiver UART to \SI{57600}{bps} and apply persistent runtime settings:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Constellations:} enable GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and BeiDou search.
    \item \textit{SBAS:} enable WAAS/EGNOS-class augmentation.
    \item \textit{AIC:} enable Active Interference Cancellation for narrow-band RFI.
    \item \textit{NMEA output:} restrict to RMC, GGA, GST, ZDA and channel-status, at an update period of \SI{200}{ms} (5\,Hz).
    \item \textit{Fix timing:} align fix/update control to the chosen period and disable UART saturation modes that could induce backlog at high rates.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Re-arm the local TTY:} reconfigure \texttt{/dev/serial0} to \SI{57600}{bps} (raw, 8N1) for the new link speed.
  \item \textbf{PPS permissions and start:} ensure PPS device access, then launch \texttt{gpsd} in foreground with \texttt{-n} and \texttt{-s 57600} bound to \texttt{/dev/serial0}.
\end{enumerate}


\noindent The figure \ref{fig:gps_service_flow} presents a sequence diagram illustrating the interactions between the service, the GNSS receiver, and the operating system during initialization.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/C4/gps_config.pdf}
    \caption{Boot-time GNSS service: sequence diagram. The unit frees the TTY, negotiates \SI{57600}{bps}, applies GNSS/NMEA profile (5\,Hz; RMC/GGA/GST/ZDA/CHN; SBAS; AIC; multi-constellation), and launches \texttt{gpsd}.}
    \label{fig:gps_service_flow}

\noindent This design eliminates race conditions between the OS and the receiver at power-up, prevents stale baud settings, and reduces NMEA traffic to only the sentences consumed by the acquisition software. The 5\,Hz profile balances latency and CPU load on the Raspberry~Pi while preserving timing fidelity for georeferencing and overlay rendering.

\subsection{Automatic video upload}
\label{subsec:cloud_sync}

To ensure that data are preserved and available for downstream processing without manual intervention, a pair of \texttt{systemd} units implements a robust, network-aware synchronization workflow to cloud storage. Specifically, an Azure Blob Storage account was configured, with infrastructure dedicated exclusively to synchronizing the images captured by the HuMath QBee devices (see Figure~\ref{fig:video_sync_arch}):


\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Bulk sync at boot (\texttt{oneshot}):} after \texttt{network-online.target}, a sync job scans the local \texttt{videos/} directory, enumerates all \texttt{*.avi} assets, and uploads only the files absent from the remote container. A local manifest (\texttt{.uploaded\_manifest.txt}) caches the known remote inventory to avoid re-listing on subsequent runs.
  \item \textbf{Event-driven uploads (\texttt{.path} + templated \texttt{@.service}):} a file-system watcher observes modifications in \texttt{videos/}. Each new or closed file triggers a one-shot upload of that specific asset. This avoids delays between acquisition and offloading and reduces bandwidth spikes compared to monolithic pushes.
  \item \textbf{Addressing and idempotency:} destination object keys follow a canonical scheme
        \[
        \texttt{YYYY/MM/DD/raw/qbee/\textit{filename}.avi}
        \]
        derived from the timestamp embedded in the filename (e.g.\\ \texttt{video\_20250217\_113221.avi}). Before each upload, the client checks the manifest (or, for single-file mode, queries the container) to ensure idempotent behavior.
  \item \textbf{Credentialing and isolation:} credentials and parameters (account, key, container, local directory) are injected via an \texttt{EnvironmentFile} (\texttt{.env}); the sync client runs in a dedicated virtual environment to pin dependencies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dependencies and Execution Environment}

The system operates on a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B running Raspberry Pi OS (Debian-based), with the software developed in Python 3.8. The core functionality, image acquisition, is designed to be robust and independent of other services, relying on the \texttt{vmbpy}, \texttt{opencv-python}, \texttt{numpy}, \texttt{threading}, \texttt{queue}, and \texttt{keyboard} libraries. Two secondary services, geolocation and cloud synchronization, have their own dependencies (\texttt{gpsd-py3} and \texttt{azure-storage-blob}, \texttt{python-dotenv}, respectively). The image acquisition process continues to run unaffected if the GPS module fails to get a fix or if the internet connection is unavailable. However, for these secondary services to function correctly, certain conditions must be met. Cloud synchronization requires a stable internet connection. Correct georeferencing of the images is contingent on the GPS module obtaining a "fix," which is subject to a variety of external factors, including atmospheric conditions and satellite visibility, and is not solely dependent on the module's configuration. Visualization is performed through the Raspberry’s HDMI output, and the environment variable \texttt{DISPLAY=":0"} must be set to enable OpenCV’s GUI output.

\subsection{Remote Operation of the \texttt{QBee} Device}

In addition to its autonomous operational capabilities in the field, the \texttt{QBee} system is equipped with functionalities for remote control. This allows for the monitoring and operation of the device from external platforms such as personal computers, tablets, or smartphones, facilitating both laboratory testing and in-field operational adjustments. The system supports two primary methods for accessing the graphical user interface of the HuMath\textregistered\ QBee:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Local Area Network (LAN) Access via VNC:} This method requires establishing a Virtual Network Computing (VNC) connection to the Raspberry~Pi~4 that powers the \texttt{QBee} device. The Raspberry Pi is configured to automatically join a designated Wi-Fi network. For a successful connection, the control device must reside on the same LAN. Access is granted using the device's IP address or a pre-configured static hostname, secured with user credentials.

    \item \textbf{Global Internet Access via Raspberry Pi Connect:} This method utilizes Raspberry Pi Connect, a service that enables secure, browser-based access to the device's graphical interface from any location with an internet connection. To enable this functionality, the device must be registered to a Raspberry~Pi account and have access to a known Wi-Fi network.
\end{enumerate}


\noindent This remote-control capability enables real-time supervision and the capture of specific events without requiring physical interaction with the device. Furthermore, the \texttt{QBee} system is designed to receive remote code updates. New features, bug fixes, and other software improvements can be deployed via \texttt{git} from a private GitHub repository, ensuring a continuous cycle of development and enhancement.


\section{Operational Tests}
  
The test scenarios defined to validate the proper functioning of the \textit{QBee} system are classified into two main groups: (i) ground tests under controlled conditions, and (ii) flight tests.

\subsection{Ground tests}
    
Before any aerial deployment, it is essential to perform ground tests that allow the system to be validated under controlled conditions, without displacement and without exposure to extreme environments. In this context, an extreme environment is defined as any setting with conditions that fall outside the typical ranges for Colombia's diverse main cities. This includes temperatures significantly exceeding the 17--31~\si{\celsius} range common in most populous areas, relative humidity persistently above 85\%, the presence of rain, or challenging nighttime lighting conditions \cite{weatherspark_colombia_climate}.\\

        
\noindent Since the \textit{QBee} system does not have an active illumination source, tests are carried out exclusively during the day, taking advantage of sunlight as the main source for acquiring multispectral images. The objective of these tests is to verify the system's general behavior under normal operating conditions. In particular, the following are evaluated:
        
\begin{itemize}
\item The correct automatic initialization of the acquisition script when the device is powered on.
\item The image capture functionality when pressing the \texttt{s} key.
\item Video recording and its stoppage using the \texttt{v} key.
\item The device’s stability when operating for long periods of time.
\end{itemize}
        
\noindent The tests were conducted using two forms of power supply: a \textbf{direct source} via a USB-C adapter connected to a conventional power outlet, and an \textbf{internal source} using the rechargeable battery integrated into the \textit{QBee} housing.\\
        
\noindent One of the most relevant tests consisted of capturing an image using the \texttt{s} key, either from a keyboard connected directly to the Raspberry Pi 4 or via remote access. \subsection{Surface tests under extreme conditions}

The 7\textsuperscript{th} Air Campaign and 11\textsuperscript{th} Scientific Expedition to Antarctica, organized by the Colombian Air Force \cite{FAC_Antartida_2025}, took place between December 2024 and March 2025 on the Antarctic Peninsula. The expedition operated from various international scientific bases, including those of Chile, Argentina, and Peru. This campaign provided a unique opportunity to subject the QBee system to the extreme and highly variable climatic conditions of the austral summer in Antarctica. Operating at average daytime temperatures that, although mild for the continent, fluctuated between \SI{-6}{\celsius} and \SI{2}{\celsius}, and with near-constant daylight of 18 to 24 hours \cite{SeasonsYear_Antarctica}, the system's performance was evaluated to determine its reliability in cold environments.\\

\noindent The system exhibited intermittent operational failures under these conditions. The most common issues were startup failures and an inability to initiate video recording. These malfunctions are likely attributable to two main factors: inadequate initialization of the Raspberry Pi 4 processing system at low temperatures, and temperature-induced performance degradation of the VIS and NIR sensors.\\

\noindent Despite these challenges, successful video captures were recorded, demonstrating the system's potential to function in extreme cold. These partial successes suggest that with targeted hardware and software adjustments, the QBee's performance in hostile environments can be significantly improved. Future work should focus on a detailed root cause analysis of the observed failures. Key areas of investigation should include the thermal tolerance of the electronic components and the development of robust initialization routines for cold-weather operations.\\

\noindent While the current prototype is not suitable for regular operational missions in Antarctica, this expedition yielded invaluable data. The findings underscore the need for specific design modifications, such as improved thermal insulation, the selection of components with wider operating temperature ranges, and the implementation of pre-heating or temperature-aware initialization procedures. These lessons are critical for the development of future iterations of the QBee system, enabling its deployment in a wider range of challenging environmental monitoring scenarios.\\

\subsection{Flight tests}

The flight tests of the \textit{QBee} system were made possible thanks to collaboration with the Colombian Air Force, which facilitated installation of the device on different aircraft in its fleet. In a first test, the \textit{QBee} unit was installed aboard a Cessna 208 Caravan aircraft, performing a flight at an altitude of approximately 5{,}000 feet. The flight took place under ideal conditions, with a heading and behavior typical of a commercial route and without significant atmospheric disturbances. Throughout the journey, the system operated stably and without issues.\\

\noindent As part of this test, the system’s remote monitoring capability was verified using a Wi-Fi network enabled inside the aircraft, through which the Raspberry Pi’s graphical environment was accessed using the VNC protocol. This connection made it possible to observe the system’s data acquisition in real time, confirming full script functionality and sensor responsiveness. \noindent The analysis of angular and linear velocity obtained from GPS logs and recordings during flights aboard the T-27 Tucano confirmed that the mechanical mount withstands moderate vibrations and accelerations of up to 4g without loss of alignment or acquisition dropouts.

\noindent The operational test results reveal that the system is highly dependent on ambient lighting conditions, as expected. The test on the T-27 Tucano was conducted between 17:30 and 18:45, where the lighting conditions are particularly complex, as is evident from the captured frames. Similarly, it is observable that the results for the NIR channel were superior to those for the VIS channel. This phenomenon is attributed to the atmospheric scattering effects prevalent at sunset, which lead to a greater availability of illumination in the NIR spectrum compared to the VIS spectrum as evening progresses.\\

\noindent In both flight tests, the system behaved adequately, demonstrating that the services and software developed for image capture function correctly. It must be clarified that in both tests, the optical systems were not properly focused, resulting in blurry acquired images. The optical system in question will be analyzed in a subsequent chapter.\\
\section{Conclusions and Lessons Learned}

\noindent The procurement and construction process of the HuMath\textregistered~QBee successfully materialized a lightweight, robust, and fully functional multispectral payload within the mass (1,810 g) and volume margins imposed by the C3 UAV platform, demonstrating that PET-G manufacturing and modular integration of VIS and NIR sensors can meet university aerospace requirements without resorting to external suppliers. The developed mechanical, electrical, and control architecture enabled autonomous and remote operation of the system, ensuring synchronization of images and metadata at a frame rate of up to 24 FPS.\\


\noindent The experimental campaigns showed that ambient temperature is the most critical operational factor: between 10~\si{\celsius} and 40~\si{\celsius} the device operated without incidents, whereas at 0~\si{\celsius} in Antarctica intermittent initialization failures, underexposure of the VIS sensor, and electronic saturation problems in the Raspberry Pi were observed. These anomalies suggest that passive thermal insulation elements and hardware pre-heating strategies should be incorporated for missions in cold climates. In contrast, under intense solar radiation on the ground, levels close to 90\,\% saturation were reached in the NIR sensor; therefore, it is recommended to add automatic gain control and interchangeable ND filters to preserve dynamic range.\\

%   \noindent The analysis of angular and linear velocity obtained from GPS logs and recordings during flights aboard the T-27 Tucano confirmed that the mechanical mount withstands moderate vibrations and accelerations without loss of alignment or acquisition dropouts. The complete video of these tests, along with representative frames, is archived in the institutional repository (permanent links appear in Annex B), which ensures the traceability of results and facilitates future scientific audits.\\

\noindent At the software level, the multithreaded script based on the \texttt{vmbpy} library proved stable for more than 30 continuous hours of operation, but tests in extreme environments revealed bottlenecks when the buffer reached memory saturation. As improvement actions, it is recommended to migrate to a producer–consumer architecture based on \texttt{asyncio} to reduce latency, implement a \textit{watchdog} to restart frozen services, and enable hardware video compression to reduce the load on the Raspberry Pi 4 CPU.\\

\noindent Finally, collaboration with the Colombian Air Force and the participation of volunteer students from various universities highlighted the value of inter-institutional agreements. The lessons learned here—thermal insulation, saturation control, optical focusing, acquisition-rate management, and thorough documentation of code and data—lay the groundwork for developing HuMath\textregistered~QBee versions capable of operating reliably in even more challenging climatic scenarios and expanding their reach in national and international environmental monitoring programs.\\
